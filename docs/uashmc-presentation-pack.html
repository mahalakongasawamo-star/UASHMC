Section 1

The case in one page

UASHMC opened in 2025 and is still building awareness, credibility and census. It has no website. Everything a prospective patient can currently find is either on Facebook or on somebody else's listing.

The story, in five lines

  • UASHMC needs visibility and patient census.
  • Patients need a credible destination they can reach at 10pm on a phone.
  • The website becomes UASHMC's digital front door — structured, owned, machine-readable.
  • KRISS AI makes that front door answer questions 24/7, in the patient's own words.
  • The same content serves the page, Google, and the assistant. One content effort, three payoffs.

Three verified facts worth saying out loud

Competitive

We found no Philippine hospital with a real assistant on its own site

In a sample of eleven Philippine hospital websites checked in August 2026, none had on-site chat. St. Luke's, Makati Med, The Medical City and Asian Hospital all push patients into Facebook Messenger instead — Asian Hospital's named "AI assistant" is a Messenger link. Say it as a dated sample, never as an absolute: one counter-example undoes an absolute.

Urgency

Six of ten Antipolo private hospitals have no website at all

And one of the four that does was returning server errors on every path when we checked. Handle that last point carefully in the room — it is a Metro Pacific property, and they are a potential partner as easily as a rival. The argument is that the bar is low, not that a named competitor is failing.

Opportunity

The bar in Antipolo is on the floor

Antipolo Doctors Hospital is the one to beat, and it has no doctor search at all. Prices live in images and a SharePoint spreadsheet. Eight weeks of focused work wins the catchment.

What we are asking for on the day

Not approval of a design. Agreement on direction, a named project owner, a photo shoot date, a domain decision, and permission to start content gathering. The nine specific asks are in Section 11.

The homepage concept

A clickable prototype, not a picture of one. Desktop and mobile, six pages, a working HMO checker, and a live KRISS AI demo you can type into during the meeting.

Open the prototype

Runs offline from a laptop. Switch between desktop and mobile in the top bar, and toggle Show content sources to see exactly which facts came from UASHMC and which are ours.

Open concept →

Two directions, not one — pick with us

The prototype ships two homepage directions, switchable in the top bar. Both keep UASHMC's tagline. The difference is structural — what the hospital leads with:

  • A · Task-first. The wayfinding board is the hero. No photography above the fold. Leads with utility: tell us what you need and we'll get you there.
  • B · Story-first. A full-bleed photograph of the hospital with the tagline over it, two calls to action, and the six tasks as a compact strip immediately below. Leads with presence: this is a real place, and here is what it feels like.

This is a genuine marketing decision with real consequences, and it is Harvey's to make. Showing one finished design invites critique; showing two invites a choice.

The one idea the homepage is built on

A hospital homepage is usually a brochure: a photo of the building, a mission statement, and a news feed. That answers a question nobody asked. The question a prospective patient actually arrives with is "can this hospital handle what I need, and what do I do next?"

So the hero is not a photo. It is a wayfinding board — the hospital's lobby directory, made digital. Six real patient intents, above the fold, one tap each: Go to the ER now · Find a doctor · Check my HMO · Services & tests · Admission requirements · How to get here. Everything else on the page supports those six.

The honest-placeholder layer — a feature, not an apology

The prototype has a toggle that highlights every fact on the page by where it came from: green for things we took from UASHMC's own public channels, amber for plausible things you need to confirm, and violet for Iozera placeholders that need your content.

Two reasons this matters. It means we never accidentally imply we invented hospital facts. And when you flip it on in the meeting, the highlighted items are the content checklist — the conversation produces the to-do list by itself.

Design decisions worth defending, with the evidence

The assistant does not auto-open

Baymard's usability testing found all three common live-chat implementations actively disruptive, and sticky chat especially damaging on mobile where it hides controls with no way to move it. For a hospital, quiet is a trust signal. The metric to optimise is resolution rate among people who opened it — never open rate.

On mobile it is a peer of calling, not a bubble over it

A sticky bottom bar with Call · Book · Ask as equals. Nothing floats over content, the call button can never be occluded, and the assistant sits in the thumb zone where primary actions belong — so it gets more prominence than a floating bubble, with less friction.

No stock photography of people

For a hospital whose existence a patient cannot yet confirm from reputation, stock imagery is net negative — it reads as a hospital that won't show itself. Every photo slot in the prototype is a labelled placeholder and a line item in the shot list.

We don't publish the doctor count

Mount Elizabeth says "more than 850 doctors" because scale is their claim. UASHMC's claim is different. A complete 20-doctor directory with photos, schedules and HMO tags reads far better than a 40-doctor list where half are stubs. Completeness reads as competence; headcount doesn't.

Four decisions we deliberately left open for Harvey

  1. How much price to publish — indicative room rates and package ranges, or process only? Real commercial call with a real maintenance cost.
  2. The opening prompt set — we proposed four. Harvey knows what people actually ask on Messenger today. That is primary research we don't have.
  3. The service catalogue's front door — specialty-first, or concern-first ("Heart & circulation")?
  4. The three mobile bar slots — Call · Book · Ask · Directions: which three earn a patient's thumb?

The architectural argument — why "KRISS AI-ready from Day One" is real

Every page marked KB is written as structured, dated, semantic content with a named owner. That single body of content is simultaneously the web page, the SEO corpus, the corpus that ChatGPT and Google's AI answers can retrieve, and the grounding data KRISS AI answers from.

There is never a separate chatbot content project. That is what makes this an architecture decision rather than a bolt-on — and it is why the assistant can be trusted: it can only say what the hospital has already approved and published.

Integrate, don't duplicate

  • NowServing.ph — keep it as the booking engine. Every doctor profile deep-links to that doctor's NowServing page. We do not rebuild booking.
  • Facebook & Instagram — remain the top-of-funnel. The site is where those posts land.
  • "Doctor in the House" — already-produced, doctor-fronted, hospital-approved video. Embed episodes on the matching doctor and service pages. It makes a small roster look substantially deeper on day one.

Ask UASHMC · 1:22 AM · the moment that matters

Yes — UASHMC has an accreditation agreement with Maxicare. (HMO list as of 12 Aug 2026.)

That's the hospital-level answer, and it's the easy part. I want to be straight with you about the rest, because this is where bills go wrong.

Four things still decide whether your specific surgery is covered, and I can't confirm any of them:

  • Your plan. Coverage for a planned surgery is set by your plan, not by the hospital.
  • Your doctor. Maxicare keeps its own accredited-doctor list. Practising here doesn't put a doctor on it.
  • The procedure. A planned surgery normally needs an approved LOA before admission.
  • Outpatient vs inpatient vs emergency. These work differently.

The one thing I'd flag now: request the LOA as soon as your doctor recommends the procedure. It typically takes 1–5 business days, and this is the single most common reason admissions get postponed.

Safety, demonstrated rather than asserted

If anyone pushes on clinical risk, these are the moments in the transcripts to point at:

  • Never diagnoses. No symptom question is ever asked, in any journey.
  • Red flags interrupt, they don't converse. Chest pain replaces the answer with an emergency card. No "are you sure?", no severity question, no offer to book.
  • Doesn't re-assess when the patient de-escalates. KRISS never concludes it is safe.
  • HMO answered accurately, not naively. Agreement at hospital level, then four caveats and a terminal instruction to verify with both the HMO and the hospital desk.
  • No personal financial estimates. Package ranges with exclusions and a date — then an explicit refusal to compute what this patient pays.
  • Refuses live operational data. Room availability: "if I gave you a number it could be wrong by tomorrow morning."
  • Honest "I don't know" with a real handoff. Every unknown is logged.
  • Every answer traceable to an approved page and its update date. Wrong answers become fixable content, not mysterious model behaviour.

Section 6

How KRISS AI fits

Not two products bolted together. The website is what makes the assistant trustworthy; the assistant is what makes the website work at 11pm.

Where it appears

Desktop

Bottom-right, 24px inset. A quiet 56–64px launcher labelled "Ask UASHMC" — labelled for its job, not its technology. Collapsed by default, never auto-opening. Expanded it is ~400px wide and does not modal-block the page. A second entry point sits in the footer, where people expect to find help.

Mobile

Folded into a sticky bottom bar as one of three peers: Call · Book · Ask. Full-screen when open. Suppressed entirely on the emergency page and on any form. The call button can never be occluded — that is a hard rule, not a preference.

The safety model, as rules an engineer can implement

  1. Identifies itself as an AI system in the widget, before first input.
  2. Answers only from hospital-approved content. No generative clinical inference.
  3. No diagnosis, no clinical advice, no triage, no symptom assessment. This is a regulatory boundary, not only a policy: under FDA AO 2018-0002, software classification turns on intended use. The guardrail keeps the system outside the medical-device perimeter and protects the hospital's licence exposure.
  4. Emergency intent triggers an unconditional interrupt to 911, the trunkline and the ER. No conversation, no severity assessment, no confirmation gate. Bias toward over-escalation.
  5. HMO and PhilHealth: may state whether an agreement exists and what to bring. May never state that a service will be covered, quote an amount, or confirm a specific doctor is accredited under a specific plan.
  6. Physician information: credentials only, never comparative or superiority claims.
  7. A named human escalation path and a grievance mechanism, always one tap away.
  8. Chat logs are sensitive personal information — declared retention, access controls, a deletion route, and honouring objection and erasure requests.
  9. A privacy impact assessment before launch, and a standing disclaimer that is never dismissed after first read.

The liability point to volunteer before anyone asks

Under NPC Advisory 2024-04, accountability for an AI system sits with UASHMC as Personal Information Controller. It cannot be delegated to Iozera or to the technology. Saying that first is the credibility move — it converts the conversation from "a chatbot" into "a governed system with named owners", which is the register Dr. Pedraja and Dr. Directo will respond to.

What the hospital gets back — the insight loop

Every unanswered question is logged. That log is a monthly report: the top inquiries, the questions with no approved answer, and demand signals for services UASHMC doesn't offer yet. It is continuous market research on the Antipolo catchment, and it is naturally Harvey's asset.

Three limitations in the room. The rest in the pack.

Twelve caveats delivered to a board before they have agreed in principle turns diligence into doubt. Say exactly these three out loud, and leave the rest in writing:

  1. It only knows what you publish. That is the design, and it is why the content work matters more than the technology.
  2. It does not diagnose. Ever. By design — not by good behaviour.
  3. You would be the first hospital deployment anywhere. Say it before they find it; volunteered it is diligence, discovered it is a problem.

The rule that governs the whole meeting: any weakness you volunteer is diligence. Any weakness they discover is a lie.

The full limitations list — for the leave-behind, not the room

  • Website widget only at v1. Not Messenger, not Viber, not SMS, not voice. UASHMC's patients live on Facebook and Viber, so expect this question — and answer it as scope, not capability.
  • Filipino and Taglish must be tested, not assumed.
  • It does not book appointments. It deep-links into NowServing, which UASHMC already runs. This is the honest framing and it makes the existing investment look deliberate.
  • No EMR, HIS or HMO system integration. Not in scope at v1.
  • Pilot on three journeys first — HMO, hours and directions, service information — and expand on evidence rather than ambition.

The flagship framing

No hospital anywhere has publicly deployed KRISS AI. Rather than borrow a logo, offer Dr. Bravo something better: UASHMC as the flagship hospital deployment. It is true, it is more compelling than a reference he can't verify, and it is something a president building a new institution's reputation may actively want.

Calendar honesty

Add two weeks of buffer if the schedule crosses Holy Week, Christmas and New Year, or the Antipolo fiesta calendar. Philippine approval cycles stop dead in those windows, and pretending otherwise is how a plan loses credibility in week nine.

Why we recommend the fast path

  1. It beats every hospital website in Antipolo in eight weeks. Six of ten have no site, and the nearest same-corridor competitor is currently offline.
  2. It turns the content problem from a blocker into a rhythm. Enriching a live site is a monthly habit. Finishing all content before launch is a cliff.
  3. The "first Philippine hospital with a real on-site assistant" claim has a shelf life. Twelve weeks protects it. Twenty-one risks it.

The governing principle

Publish thin, enrich in public. A page is either complete and live, or absent — never live and half-empty. That single rule removes the most common reason a launch slips.

A named owner with authority · a weekly 30-minute standing call (thirty, not sixty — short enough that it never gets cancelled) · publish thin then enrich · template-level clinical approval with review by exception · a shared decision log with a 5-working-day default approval for staging
2 Clinical sign-off bottleneck. Dr. Pedraja is a practising clinician, and is simultaneously the most engaged stakeholder and the most likely bottleneck. We draft, she edits · approve two templates once, then review by exception · two pre-booked 90-minute review blocks rather than an open email thread · a named clinical deputy for routine profiles · hand her the guardrail spec as a finished artifact to countersign
3 Photography: no assets, no date, no releases. The longest physical lead time, and the one most often assumed away. Without headshots, Find a Doctor cannot launch — and that is the module that beats ADH. Book the shoot date in the room, today · we send the shot list, headshot spec and release form within two working days · run a half-day headshot station across two or three days to catch clinic schedules
4 Doctor roster data quality and schedule volatility. Wrong schedules generate complaints; absent schedules generate phone calls. A written completeness gate · a named schedule owner with a monthly cadence · publish typical clinic days labelled "confirm when booking" rather than false precision · one canonical provider record that the site and KRISS both read
5 Domain and DNS not owned, or credentials unobtainable. The quietest schedule-killer — a launch-ready site sitting on staging because nobody can find who registered the domain. Resolve on day one · if unowned, we register on UASHMC's behalf with a signed transfer letter under a UASHMC-owned mailbox · verify DNS access in week 2, not launch week
6 HMO, PhilHealth and price content — the highest-harm content on the site. A confident wrong answer here produces a family with an unexpected bill. Publish as "HMOs we have accreditation agreements with", never "HMOs we accept" · a visible last-updated date with a named owner · the four caveats on every answer · split outpatient / inpatient / emergency · Billing signs off every money page in writing
7 Scope creep into patient portal, native booking, EMR integration. "While we're at it, can it also show lab results?" A written Phase 1 boundary with a Phase 2 candidate list, so nothing is refused — only sequenced · deep-link to NowServing rather than rebuilding booking
8 Too many reviewers, conflicting feedback. Marketing wants concern-first navigation; Medical wants departments. Both are legitimate. One consolidated feedback channel through the named owner · two review rounds included, the third is a change order · resolve department-vs-concern structurally: one record set, three indexes over it, so both get their door
9 Board or governance review cycle. If the board must ratify scope and meets monthly, an unaligned cycle adds 3–4 weeks with no work being done. Ask directly on the day whether Dr. Bravo can approve Phase 1 without ratification. If not, get the board date and ask Dr. Directo what the board will want to see, so we prepare it rather than discover it.
10 Front-desk readiness at launch. The site goes live and nobody answering the phone knows what it says. A 45-minute staff briefing in launch week for front desk, admitting, HMO/billing and the ER station, plus a one-page "report a correction" route

Section 10

Discovery questions

Designed to surface decisions, not opinions. Authority and ownership first, because everything downstream depends on them. We are not arriving assuming we know what UASHMC wants.

01Dr. BravoDecision authority and budget
"Walk us through how a decision like this actually gets made at UASHMC. Can you approve the Phase 1 scope and spend today, or does it need board ratification — and if so, when does the board next sit? And is there a date driving this: an anniversary, an accreditation milestone, a census target?"

Determines whether we're planning an 8-week project or a 12-week one with a governance gate in the middle. The launch-date question is the one agencies forget to ask and then discover in week six.

02Dr. Bravo + HarveyWhat success looks like
"Twelve months after this site is live, what has changed that makes you say it was worth it? Census, HMO patient mix, inbound calls that arrive already informed, doctor recruitment — or something else? And what's the six-month version?"

Census, referral diversification, HMO share and physician recruitment produce materially different homepages. Nobody has told us which. Asking makes the project accountable rather than decorative — and it becomes the measurement plan.

03Dr. Bravo + TereDomain and email ownership
"Does UASHMC own a domain today, and who holds the registrar login? And separately — your published contact address is a Gmail account. Patient inquiries with health information are flowing into a consumer mailbox. Can we agree today to stand up hospital-domain mailboxes, and who can make that happen?"

The quietest schedule-killer and simultaneously the cheapest large credibility win. Framing it as security remediation rather than branding makes it an action item this week.

04Tere BarcelonaThe named owner and operating rhythm
"We need one person who owns this from your side — who can consolidate feedback, chase content, and say yes. Who is that, and can we put a standing 30-minute call in the calendar right now? And who will be the day-to-day content steward once the site is live — the person who updates the HMO list and the clinic schedules?"

The mitigation for the #1 risk, and it must be settled in the room. A project with five stakeholders and no owner does not slip — it stalls. If no name emerges, that is the most important finding of the day.

05Dr. PedrajaThe clinical approval mechanism
"Everything clinical needs your signature. Rather than sending you a hundred approvals, we'd like to approve the physician profile template and the service page template once, then handle individual pages by exception. Does that work, and what review turnaround can you realistically commit to? Would two booked 90-minute sessions be easier than an email thread?"

Turns the bottleneck into a schedule, and respects that she is a practising clinician — which she will notice. Bring the draft template with you. Worth noting to her: the PRC/PMA Code of Ethics expressly permits websites to publish credentials, post-graduate training and former hospital connections, on the reasoning that a patient has the right to know their doctor's qualifications. A substantive doctor directory is the more ethical choice for a website, not the riskier one.

06Dr. Pedraja, with Dr. BravoThe guardrails and who owns the review loop
"We've written the guardrail spec for KRISS AI before you asked for it: hospital-approved information only, no diagnosis, no clinical advice, no triage, no symptom questions, and an unconditional interrupt to 911 and your ER on any red-flag term. Here it is. What would you add or remove — and will you own the monthly review of the red-flag trigger log?"

Decides whether KRISS AI is a governance conversation or a novelty conversation. Volunteering the constraint — and the liability — is the credibility move with two clinicians and a board member in the room.

07Harvey LozadaWhat patients actually ask
"You see the real questions every day on Messenger, Viber and Instagram. What are the top twenty, in the words people actually use — including the Taglish? And which ones do you find yourself answering over and over?"

Primary research we cannot get anywhere else, and the fastest quality win available. It writes the homepage task row, the search synonyms, the navigation labels and the assistant's opening prompts. It also positions Harvey as a source of expertise rather than an approver of our work.

08Harvey LozadaBrand, photography and the shoot date
"What brand assets exist in editable form — vector logo, exact colour values, fonts? And here's our shot list. For a hospital nobody has heard of yet, stock photos of people actively hurt us — they read as a hospital that won't show itself. So we need real photography: the façade from both directions so drivers recognise you, the ER entrance separately, rooms by type, the imaging suite, real staff, and a headshot of every published doctor. When can we shoot?"

The longest physical lead time on the project. A date in the calendar is worth more than any other single commitment of the day.

09Harvey + Dr. BravoThe transparency posture
"How much do you want to publish — room rates, package prices, indicative ranges, or process only? Here's the market reality: Chong Hua publishes fifteen room rates with photos. In Antipolo, ADH put 'Our Packages' first in its navigation. And third-party aggregators are already publishing hospital rates with worse accuracy and zero accountability. The question isn't whether your prices get published. It's whether you own the answer."

A genuine business decision, correctly theirs. Note honestly that the DOH price-transparency rule speaks to physical conspicuous areas; whether it reaches websites is unsettled. So online transparency is a differentiator, not an obligation — and a stale price list is worse than none.

10Tere, with Dr. BravoRecords, licences and the operational truth
"We need five documents and five facts. The DOH License to Operate — registered name, classification level, authorised services, validity. Your PhilHealth accreditations by benefit package, including Konsulta. Your Zero Balance Billing policy as it actually applies, in writing from Billing. Your DPO's name and whether your data processing systems are registered with the NPC. And your NowServing admin access. Who holds each, and when can we have them?"

Every one is a launch blocker or a compliance exposure, and each has a different holder inside the hospital. Asking as one bundled list produces one owner and one date rather than five chases over six weeks.

Reserve questions, if the room allows

  • Dr. Bravo: "Where are you losing patients today, and why — ADH, Marikina Valley, or straight past Antipolo into Manila?"
  • Dr. Directo: "When this reaches the board, what will they want to see?"
  • Tere: "Is there an HIS, EMR or lab system in place, and any appetite to connect them later? We're not scoping it — we just want to know what we're building next to."
  • Anyone: "Do you have a direct ER line separate from the trunkline? If not, can we get one provisioned?" — a one-day operational change with outsized effect, and only two hospitals in the entire competitive sample get this right.

Section 12

Suggested agenda

Ninety minutes. The prototype does most of the talking, and one full block belongs to Harvey rather than to us.

The strongest opening move — do not lead with our research

"Doc, may I borrow your phone for one minute? I'd like you to try to find out — using only what exists online today — whether UASHMC has a cardiologist, and whether you take Maxicare."

Let him fail, in front of his own executive team, in ninety seconds. Then take the phone back and do it in four taps on the prototype. That turns every claim in this pack into felt experience before you have said one word about yourselves.

Have a 60-second screen recording as backup in case the wifi is dead or he would rather not play along. Opening with the competitor audit instead is the vendor talking about his homework — a fine second move, a weak first one.
